<h1>How to Plan Your First Trip to China from New Zealand: A Step-by-Step Guide</h1>

<p>Planning your first trip to China from New Zealand is genuinely one of the most rewarding things you can do with your travel budget — but it does take a bit more groundwork than booking a quick jaunt to Bali. The short answer: start with your visa, pick one or two regions rather than trying to cover everything, travel in shoulder season, and seriously consider going with a specialist operator who has direct on-ground connections in China. That last point is where I've seen the biggest difference for Kiwi travellers over the years. Here at <strong>CTS Tours NZ</strong>, we've been helping New Zealanders navigate exactly this journey for decades, and the steps below are what we walk every first-timer through.</p>

<h2>Step 1: Sort Your Visa (It's Easier Than You Think)</h2>

<p>The China visa is the first thing that stops people in their tracks — but it genuinely shouldn't. As a New Zealand passport holder, you currently have access to China's 15-day visa-free entry policy for tourism, which opened up significantly in recent years. That means for many standard itineraries, you won't need to visit a consulate at all.</p>

<p>If you're planning a longer trip — say, three weeks or more — you'll want a tourist visa (L visa), which requires an application through the Chinese Visa Application Service Centre. You'll need a completed form, your passport, a recent photo, and proof of onward travel and accommodation. Processing typically takes four to five business days. Our team at CTS Tours NZ handles this briefing for every client, because the requirements do shift, and getting the paperwork wrong is an avoidable headache.</p>

<h2>Step 2: Choose the Right Time of Year to Go</h2>

<p>China is vast — almost 40 times the size of New Zealand — which means the "best time to visit" varies enormously by region. That said, for first-timers, two windows stand out:</p>

<ul>
  <li><strong>April to May (spring):</strong> Mild temperatures, cherry blossoms in many northern cities, and manageable crowds before the summer rush.</li>
  <li><strong>September to October (autumn):</strong> This is arguably the sweet spot. Golden light, cooler temperatures, and the landscapes — particularly places like Guilin, Yunnan, and the Great Wall — are at their most photogenic. Crowds thin out after the October Golden Week holiday (avoid the first week of October itself).</li>
</ul>

<p>Mid-to-late October is a particular favourite for our groups. The light in Beijing and Xi'an at that time of year is extraordinary, and Yunnan's Dali region takes on a warmth and colour that's hard to describe until you've stood there yourself.</p>

<h2>Step 3: Build a Smart First-Timer's Itinerary</h2>

<p>The most common mistake first-timers make is trying to tick off too many cities. China rewards depth, not distance. For a first trip of 10–15 days, I'd suggest one of three approaches:</p>

<ol>
  <li><strong>The Classic North:</strong> Beijing and Xi'an — imperial history, the Great Wall, the Terracotta Warriors. Timeless for good reason.</li>
  <li><strong>The Eastern Edge:</strong> Shanghai and Suzhou — modern China alongside classical gardens, canal towns, and extraordinary food.</li>
  <li><strong>The Southwest Escape:</strong> Yunnan — Kunming, Dali, Lijiang — for those who want minority cultures, dramatic landscapes, and a slower pace.</li>
</ol>

<p>Each of these makes for a complete, satisfying first China experience without the blur of too many overnight trains and airports. <h2>Step 5: Prepare for the Practicalities</h2>

<p>A few things that catch first-timers off guard:</p>

<ul>
  <li><strong>Mobile connectivity:</strong> Google, WhatsApp, Instagram, and Facebook are blocked in China. Download a reputable VPN before you leave New Zealand (not after — you can't access most VPN websites from within China). WeChat is the local messaging app and is worth setting up before you go.</li>
  <li><strong>Payments:</strong> China is largely cashless, but the apps (WeChat Pay, Alipay) require a Chinese bank account. Many tourist sites now accept international cards or cash, but your guide will help you navigate this. On a CTS tour, this is never something you need to stress about.</li>
  <li><strong>Language:</strong> Mandarin is the dominant language, and outside major hotels and tourist sites, English is limited. This is one of the strongest arguments for travelling with a specialist rather than going independently on your first trip. Our guides are fluent in both English and Mandarin, and many have regional knowledge that even most Chinese travellers don't have.</li>
  <li><strong>Health and travel insurance:</strong> Non-negotiable. Ensure your policy covers medical evacuation — Chinese hospitals are generally excellent, but costs without insurance can be significant.</li>
</ul>

  <h3>Do New Zealand passport holders need a visa for China?</h3>
  <p>New Zealand passport holders currently benefit from China's 15-day visa-free entry policy for tourism.   <p>Spring (April–May) and autumn (mid-October onward) are the most comfortable and visually rewarding times to visit. Avoid the first week of October (China's Golden Week national holiday) when domestic crowds peak significantly.</p>
